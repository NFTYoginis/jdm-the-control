## How to Use This Document

The JDM developed original vocabulary, frameworks, and clinical tools that have broad application beyond Thai massage — particularly in Yin Yoga, somatic practice, and any movement or bodywork context. This reference gives you the core concepts in portable form.

**For Yin Yoga specifically:** The overlap is deep. Yin already works with the meridian system. The long-hold practice is a direct application of the Sensation Ladder. Concomitance explains why students feel emotions in poses. The Three Maps framework contextualises what Yin is actually doing to the body. Bio-individuality is the philosophical foundation of the whole practice.

---

## 1. The Four Words of Sensation

**The most universally applicable JDM concept.**  
A precise vocabulary for describing internal experience — replaces the vague and fear-laden word "pain."

| Word | Range | Description |
|------|-------|-------------|
| **Background** | 0–1 | Unconscious sensory noise. Your shirt on your skin. Data, not drama. |
| **Annoyed** | 2–4 | Crossed into conscious awareness. Asking for a change. The primary language of practice. |
| **Pain** | 5–7 | The negative emotional interpretation of Annoyed — tied to uncertainty, fear, or a story of damage. |
| **Injury** | 8+ | A functional declaration: "I cannot." A decision, not just a feeling. |

**In Yin Yoga context:** The long hold is designed to find the edge of Annoyed — not Pain. Teaching students this vocabulary transforms their relationship to sensation in poses. "Where is the Annoyed?" is a more useful question than "does it hurt?"

**Key insight to embed:**
> *"Pain is not the enemy. It is the opening line of a conversation."*

---

## 2. The Three Pain Guides

Three guardrails agreed before any exploratory practice begins. Applicable to Yin holds, restorative practice, any bodywork context.

1. **Intensity Guide:** Annoyed should not exceed 4 out of 10.
2. **Direction Guide:** As you move deeper, Annoyed should stay the same or reduce. If it increases — come out.
3. **Resolution Guide:** When you release the pose or pressure, the sensation should stop. Lingering irritation is information.

**In Yin Yoga context:** These three guides give students a self-regulating framework. They stop asking "how long do I hold?" and start asking "is this Annoyed staying safe?" The guides shift agency from the teacher to the student.

## 3. The Traffic Light Decision Matrix

A simple three-state clinical decision tool for any joint or movement.

| Light | Condition | Action |
|-------|-----------|--------|
| 🟢 **Green** | Full range, no discomfort | Build, load, explore freely |
| 🟡 **Yellow** | Full range with Annoyed ≤ 4, OR limited range without discomfort | Proceed with respect. Main workspace. |
| 🔴 **Red** | Limited range WITH Annoyed > 4 | De-load. Never force. Work elsewhere and return. |

**In Yin Yoga context:** Every pose entry is a Traffic Light assessment. Students who understand Green/Yellow/Red stop competing with themselves and start listening. The teacher can use this language to guide the class without prescribing one depth for everyone.

## 4. The Four Load Tributaries

The body at any moment is managing the sum of four sources of load simultaneously.

| Tributary | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Physical** | How the body is used — posture, repetition, demand, movement patterns |
| **Emotional** | The nervous system's weather — calm lake or stormy ocean |
| **Recovery** | Sleep quality — are you restored or just less tired? |
| **Nutritional** | What feeds the system doing all this work |

**The key insight:** Relief that fades quickly is almost always because one tributary is being ignored. The body's capacity isn't just physical.

**In Yin Yoga context:** A student who can't release in Yin isn't inflexible — their cup is full. A short discussion of the four loads in a Yin class transforms why students are there and what they expect from it.

**Core quote:**
> *"The cup doesn't overflow because of one thing."*

## 5. Concomitance

**The concept that physical and psychological experience are the same event in two modes — not cause and effect, but one unified reality.**

Derived from Bob Cooley's 16 Meridian Circuit system. Each meridian circuit unites a physical territory with an emotional/psychological theme. When the tissue is tight, the emotional theme is present. When the emotional theme shifts, the tissue responds.

**Key pairs for Yin Yoga:**
| Circuit | Physical Territory | Emotional Theme |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Gallbladder | Outer hip, IT band | Indecision ↔ Courage |
| Liver | Inner thigh, groin | Rigidity/frustration ↔ Flexible vision |
| Bladder | Entire posterior chain | Fear ↔ Hope, resilience |
| Kidney | Psoas, inner thigh, diaphragm | Existential exhaustion ↔ Deep reserves |
| Stomach | Hip flexors, anterior thigh | Anxiety, hunger ↔ Nourishment |
| Lung | Chest, biceps, forearm flexors | Grief ↔ Inspiration, presence |

**In Yin Yoga context:** When a student cries in pigeon pose (outer hip / Gallbladder), this is not strange — it is Concomitance. The physical release and the emotional release are the same event. Teaching this removes shame and replaces it with understanding.

**Key phrase:**
> *"The body and the mind are not partners. They are the same event."*

## 6. Bio-Individuality

**What your body needs is uniquely, precisely, unapologetically yours. No protocol, no teacher, no well-meaning advice overrides your own internal signal.**

The role of the teacher / practitioner is not to prescribe. It is to help the student hear what their own body is already saying.

**In Yin Yoga context:** This is the philosophical foundation of a mature Yin practice. Props, depth, duration — these are not standard. They are individual. A teacher who teaches this frees students from comparison and self-judgement. It also protects the teacher from the liability of prescribing what works for "everyone."

**Key quote:**
> *"Bio-individuality isn't what I want you to do. It's about what you realise your body is needing by listening to it. My job is only to help you listen to it."*

## 7. The Three Maps Framework

Three systems that together give a complete clinical picture of the human body. In any GabeYoga book, these are the three lenses.

| Map | System | The Question It Answers |
|-----|--------|------------------------|
| **Map 1** | Thai Sen Lines | WHERE is energy blocked? |
| **Map 2** | Bob Cooley's 16 Meridian Circuits | WHY is the pattern held? |
| **Map 3** | Thomas Myers' Anatomy Trains | HOW is the pattern structured? |

**In Yin Yoga context:** Yin already works with meridians (Map 2 — Paul Grilley's foundational teaching). Adding the Sen Lines (Map 1) contextualises the Thai yoga connection. Adding Anatomy Trains (Map 3) gives the structural story — why the pose targets what it targets, and what fascial line is being loaded. Together, the three maps explain Yin practice at a depth no single system can.

## 8. Relaxation vs. Therapy (The Core Distinction)

**The most important question to establish before any session or practice context.**

| Relaxation | Therapy |
|-----------|---------|
| Soothes the nervous system | Active, collaborative investigation |
| Full body, both sides evenly | Focused on a specific pattern |
| Relief lasts 1–2 days | Relief that lasts — plus understanding why |
| You receive | We discover together |

**In Yin Yoga context:** A Yin class can be either. A restorative Yin is relaxation. A targeted Yin for a specific pattern (tight hips, compressed lower back) is therapy. Teaching students to know which they need — and to choose consciously — transforms how they practice and what they get from it.

## 9. The Feedback Compass

The four questions that reveal the real-world effect of any session or practice period.

1. "How did you feel for the rest of the day after practice?"
2. "How did you sleep that night?"
3. "How did you feel 24 to 48 hours later?"
4. "What side did you wake up on?" *(sleep position = nocturnal pattern)*

**In Yin Yoga context:** These questions turn a one-time class into an ongoing investigation. Students who track these become self-aware practitioners of their own body — which is the whole point.

## 11. Vocabulary Bridge — Classical TCM → Cooley/JDM Naming

**Critical for any session writing about meridians in a yin yoga context.**

Yin yoga uses **classical TCM meridian names** (Governing Vessel, Triple Warmer, etc.). The JDM uses **Bob Cooley's 16-circuit system**, which renames and reframes two of the classical extraordinary vessels. The illustrations in the JDM project are filed under Cooley's names — not classical names.

If you look for "Triple Warmer" or "Governing Vessel" in the JDM illustration folder, you will not find them. They exist — under different names.

### For Yin Yoga Writing

When writing about the **Triple Warmer** in a yin context (classical TCM poses, meridian theory, Chapter 11 of YIN-BOOK), use the classical name in the prose. The illustration that best supports it is `Meridian-TH-thymus.png` — reference it with a note that Cooley reframes this circuit as the thymus/immune pathway.

When writing about the **Governing Vessel** (Du Mai), use the classical name in prose. The supporting illustration is `Meridian-CNS-brain.png` — reference it with a note that Cooley reframes this as the CNS/Brain circuit.

The **Skin and Appendix circuits** have no classical TCM equivalent — they are Cooley's original contribution. Do not force a yin yoga pose into them. Mention them when explaining how Cooley's system extends beyond the classical 12.

## What NOT to Port Directly

These JDM concepts are specific to the Thai massage / bodywork context and should **not** be imported unchanged into a Yin Yoga book — they would need significant reframing:

- The four-session therapeutic arc (specific to one-on-one clinical sessions)
- The practitioner worksheets (client-facing clinical tools)
- The "Relaxation or Therapy?" opening script (designed for bodywork intake)

These can **inspire** equivalent Yin concepts but should be rewritten from the ground up in Yin voice.
